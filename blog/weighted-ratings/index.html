Why a 4.9 from 40 reviews shouldn’t beat a 4.7 from 12,000.

Star averages are one of the most-used numbers on the internet, and one of the easiest to misread. Here’s the simple fix I use whenever I rank anything by ratings.

Imagine you’re choosing between two places. Shop A has 4.9 stars from 40 reviews. Shop B has 4.7 stars from 12,000 reviews. Sort by average rating and Shop A wins. But which one would you actually trust?

Most people pick Shop B, and they’re right. Forty reviews can come from friends, a good opening week or plain luck. Twelve thousand reviews are very hard to fake. The problem is that a simple average throws that difference away: it treats a rating built on 40 opinions exactly like one built on 12,000.

The idea: start every rating from the middle

The fix is called a weighted rating (or Bayesian average). Instead of trusting each average at face value, you assume every item starts close to the typical rating, and only lets its own reviews pull it away from there once it has enough of them.

The formula looks like this:

weighted = (v / (v + m)) × R + (m / (v + m)) × C
  • R is the item’s own average rating.
  • v is how many reviews it has.
  • C is the average rating across all items, the “typical” score.
  • m is how many reviews you want before you start trusting an item’s own average. Think of it as a confidence threshold.

When an item has very few reviews, m dominates and its score stays near the typical one. As reviews pile up, v takes over and the score moves towards the item’s real average.

Running the numbers

Say the typical rating across everything we’re comparing is C = 4.2, and we decide we want around m = 500 reviews before fully trusting an average. I’ve added a third shop for comparison:

Choosing m

The only real decision is how big m should be. Here’s what happens to the same three shops as you change it:

A small m trusts new items quickly; a large one is more sceptical. A practical starting point is the median number of reviews in your data: “an item needs about as many reviews as a typical item before we believe it.” Then sanity-check the top of your list and adjust.

Bonus: combining two review sites

The same thinking applies when one item has ratings on two platforms. Suppose a business has 4.8 from 300 reviews on one site and 4.2 from 2,700 on another. The lazy approach averages the two numbers and gets 4.5. But the second site holds nine times more opinions, so it should count nine times more:

(4.8 × 300 + 4.2 × 2,700) / (300 + 2,700) = 4.26

That’s a big difference: 4.26 instead of 4.5. Weighting each source by its number of reviews stops a small, enthusiastic sample from hiding what most people actually said.

In code

The whole thing fits in a few lines of Python:

def weighted_rating(R, v, C, m):
    """R = item average, v = review count,
    C = overall average, m = confidence threshold."""
    return (v / (v + m)) * R + (m / (v + m)) * C

shops = {"A": (4.9, 40), "B": (4.7, 12_000), "C": (4.4, 900)}
C, m = 4.2, 500

ranked = sorted(shops.items(),
                key=lambda s: weighted_rating(*s[1], C, m),
                reverse=True)
for name, (R, v) in ranked:
    print(name, round(weighted_rating(R, v, C, m), 2))
# B 4.68, C 4.33, A 4.25

When to use it, and when not to

  • Use it whenever you rank, sort or pick a “best” from things with very different amounts of feedback: products, apps, restaurants, suppliers, courses.
  • Show both numbers to people. Rank by the weighted score, but still display the real average and the review count, so nobody feels the data was hidden.
  • Don’t use it to judge a single item in isolation. It’s a tool for fair comparison, not a replacement for reading the reviews.

Try it on any ranking you build. Without it, tiny newcomers with a handful of perfect reviews keep floating to the top. A few lines of maths turn a misleading league table into one people can trust.
