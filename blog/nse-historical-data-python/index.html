How to get NSE stock data into Python, free.

Thirteen years of daily Nairobi Securities Exchange prices, loaded, cleaned and charted in about 40 lines of Python, including the data errors you need to fix first.

By Michael Hawi Onyango, AI engineer and SEO/GEO specialist · Data checked against public market reports

Key facts

  • Free source: daily prices for every NSE-listed stock and the main indices, January 2013 to December 2025, on Mendeley Data (CC BY 4.0).
  • Size: 222,419 daily rows across 72 listed securities (shares, preference shares, REITs and ETFs) and 10 indices, 3,228 trading days.
  • Quality: 99.5% of rows pass a day-to-day continuity check; I fixed one date typo, 69 duplicate rows and two bad index values.
  • Watch out: prices are not adjusted for splits or bonus issues.
  • Safaricom: KES 5.10 in January 2013, a KES 44.95 peak in August 2021, KES 28.35 at the end of 2025.

Most “stock market in Python” tutorials use the S&P 500, because the data is one line of code away. Kenyan data is harder to find, so most people never try. This first post in the series fixes that: where to get free NSE price history, how to load all 13 years in one go, which problems in the data to fix, and your first chart.

Where to get free NSE historical data

The best free source I found is a set of yearly files compiled by Barack Wanjawa at the University of Nairobi and published on Mendeley Data. They were originally scraped from the public MyStocks price pages for a research project on neural-network price prediction. There is one dataset per period: 2013–2020, 2021, 2022, 2023–2024 and 2025. Each one has a “Download All” button that gives you a zip of CSV files, one per year plus a list of sectors.

The licence is CC BY 4.0: you can use and share the data as long as you credit the author. All five links are in the sources at the end of this post.

What is in the files

Every row is one stock on one trading day, with 13 columns:

ColumnWhat it means
DateTrading day, e.g. 3-Jan-22
CodeTicker, e.g. SCOM for Safaricom
NameCompany name
12m Low / 12m High52-week range (unreliable in older years)
Day Low / Day HighIntraday range
Day PriceClosing price in KES
PreviousPrevious closing price
Change / Change%Move versus previous close
VolumeShares traded that day
Adjusted PriceMostly empty and unreliable; don’t use it

Index rows use a caret in the code, for example ^NASI for the NSE All Share Index and ^N20I for the NSE-20. That means the same files give you both stocks and the market as a whole.

Load 13 years in one go

Unzip the five downloads into a folder called nse-data, then run this. It is the exact code behind every number in this post.

import glob, os
import pandas as pd   # pandas 2.2 or newer

COLS = ["date", "code", "name", "low_12m", "high_12m", "day_low", "day_high",
        "close", "prev", "change", "change_pct", "volume", "adjusted"]

# 1. Load every yearly file into one table
frames = []
for path in sorted(glob.glob("nse-data/**/NSE_data_all_stocks*.csv", recursive=True)):
    year_df = pd.read_csv(path, dtype=str, encoding="utf-8-sig")
    year_df.columns = COLS
    year_df["source_file"] = os.path.basename(path)
    frames.append(year_df)
nse = pd.concat(frames, ignore_index=True)

# 2. Turn text like "1,600" or "-" into numbers
def to_number(col):
    col = col.str.replace(",", "").str.replace("%", "").str.strip()
    return pd.to_numeric(col.replace("-", None), errors="coerce")

for c in ["low_12m", "high_12m", "day_low", "day_high", "close", "prev", "volume"]:
    nse[c] = to_number(nse[c])
nse["date"] = pd.to_datetime(nse["date"].str.strip(), format="%d-%b-%y", errors="coerce")
nse["code"] = nse["code"].str.strip()

Two details save a lot of pain. First, read everything as text (dtype=str), because volumes use thousands separators and missing values are a dash. Second, use utf-8-sig, because some files start with an invisible byte-order mark that would otherwise corrupt the first column name.

Check the data before you trust it

Before charting anything, I checked for wrong dates, duplicates and values that jump and snap back. Each check found something.

  • Dates in the wrong year. The 2017 file contains one trading day stamped 09-May-15. It sits between 8 and 10 May 2017, so it is a typo for 2017.
  • Duplicates. 69 rows in the 2017 file, all dated 24 March 2017, appear twice with identical values. Drop them.
  • A value typed ten times too high. The NSE-20 index shows 18,845.31 on 26 November 2024, between days at about 1,885. Divide that one value by ten.
  • A stale index value. On 27 August 2015 the All Share Index reads 162.75 and the NSE-20 4,759.58, about 15% above the days either side, and both snap back the next day. 162.75 is exactly the All Share Index close from 15 June 2015, so it is a copied old value. Drop those two rows.
# 3. Fix the known problems
typo = (nse["source_file"] == "NSE_data_all_stocks_2017.csv") & (nse["date"] == "2015-05-09")
nse.loc[typo, "date"] = pd.Timestamp("2017-05-09")
bad = (nse["code"] == "^N20I") & (nse["date"] == "2024-11-26") & (nse["close"] > 10_000)
nse.loc[bad, "close"] = nse.loc[bad, "close"] / 10
stale = nse["code"].isin(["^NASI", "^N20I"]) & (nse["date"] == "2015-08-27")
nse = nse[~stale]
nse = (nse.dropna(subset=["date", "close"])
          .drop_duplicates(["code", "date"])
          .sort_values(["code", "date"]))

# 4. Continuity check: does "previous price" match yesterday's close?
nse["prior_close"] = nse.groupby("code")["close"].shift()
both = nse["prev"].notna() & nse["prior_close"].notna()
match = ((nse["prev"] - nse["prior_close"]).abs() / nse["prior_close"] < 0.005)[both].mean()
print(f"{len(nse):,} rows, {match:.1%} continuity")   # 222,419 rows, 99.5% continuity

Two more things to know: the files have no index values at all from January to May 2022, and 20 and 21 June 2022 look like the same day saved twice. Neither changes the yearly figures in this series, but both matter if you work with daily returns.

The continuity check is the useful one. Every row records the previous price, so for 99.5% of rows it should equal the prior row’s close. Where it doesn’t, a day is usually missing from the file. That matters if you calculate daily returns.

Finally, I compared the cleaned data against published market reports. The NSE All Share Index closes 2023 at 92.11 and the NSE-20 at 1,501.16, both exactly as reported at the time, and the All Share Index rises 51% in 2025, also matching press coverage.

The trap: splits and bonus issues

The adjusted-price column is mostly empty (it has values for part of 2013–2015 and almost none after 2018), and the values it does have don’t look reliable, so treat prices as exactly as traded. When a company issues bonus shares, its price drops overnight even though no shareholder lost money. Examples in the data line up with bonus issues at I&M Holdings (May 2019 and May 2021) and Kenya Re (June 2019 and June 2024). NSE stocks generally can’t move more than about 10% in a day, so any larger daily move deserves a second look before you use it. For this reason, the market-level posts in this series use the official indices, which already account for these events.

Your first chart: Safaricom, 2013 to 2025

# 5. Safaricom month-end prices
scom = nse[nse["code"] == "SCOM"].set_index("date")["close"]
scom.resample("ME").last().plot(title="Safaricom (SCOM), KES")

Safaricom shows why long histories matter. It rose almost ninefold, from KES 5.10 in January 2013 to KES 44.95 in August 2021. It then lost 74% of that by November 2023, to KES 11.65, in a period that included Kenya’s 2023 currency slide and heavy spending on its Ethiopia expansion. By the end of 2025 it had more than doubled from that low, to KES 28.35. Someone who only looked at 2021–2023 would draw a very different conclusion from someone who looked at the whole period.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I download historical NSE Kenya stock prices for free?

Barack Wanjawa’s NSE All Stocks Prices datasets on Mendeley Data cover every listed company and the main indices with daily prices from January 2013 to December 2025. They are free under a CC BY 4.0 licence, so you can reuse them with credit.

What columns does the NSE dataset contain?

Thirteen: date, stock code, company name, 12-month low and high, the day’s low, high and closing price, the previous price, change, change %, volume traded and an adjusted price.

Is the NSE data adjusted for stock splits and bonus issues?

No. Prices are as traded. The adjusted-price column is mostly empty and unreliable, so bonus issues and splits (for example I&M Holdings and Kenya Re) show up as sudden drops. Adjust for them before calculating long-term returns.

How accurate is the dataset?

In my checks, 99.5% of rows have a ‘previous price’ that matches the prior day’s close. Index values match published figures, such as the NSE All Share Index closing 2023 at 92.11. I found and fixed one wrong date, 69 duplicate rows and two bad index values.

What was Safaricom’s highest share price between 2013 and 2025?

KES 44.95, on 23 August 2021. It then fell to KES 11.65 on 3 November 2023 before closing 2025 at KES 28.35.
